# Coverage plan

## Rules

- Plan the scene's turn card first, then the cards leading in and out (Rabiger ch. 24).
- Density follows weight. A crucial scene gets more cards; a small moment, one or two (ch. 24).
- At the turn only, plan two or three reaction cards from different people, so the edit can pick the inflection. Elsewhere, plan only what the cut uses (ch. 24).
- Pick a relation per exchange. Isolate: separate singles, adversarial. Connect: over-the-shoulder pairs. Bind: a held two-shot. Who shares a frame shows alliances (ch. 24).
- Whose scene it is: shoot along the line between the POV character and the others. The master sits behind the POV character's shoulder when the scene is theirs. Closer to a line of tension reads more subjective (ch. 24).
- After three or more cards at CU or closer, plan one wider card to breathe (ch. 24).
- Method per scene: master, long take (one card, so only what a model holds in one generation), or short take (fast, oppositional, for peaks only) (ch. 24).
- Coverage style per scene: zone (cut between fixed areas) or man-on-man (one mover leads the camera) (Katz ch. 3).
- One dominant line of motion per scene: across, in depth, or circular. Every card's travel fits it (Katz ch. 3).
- Camera approach per scene: from one side only (cheapest, one background, no reverses), inside looking out, or opposed views. For AI, one side keeps one set and cuts location drift (Katz ch. 6).
- Put story points where a moving camera is at rest. A long moving card names its rests so the edit can cut there (Katz ch. 4-5).
- In-depth staging is easier to cut up later than lateral staging; both people share frames (ch. 4).
- Build cutaway hooks into a long take: a hand on the radio, a look out of the window (ch. 4).
- Plan the protection card in the same render batch as a risky moving card, not after review (ch. 4).
- Decide a simpler fallback staging for each complex scene before rendering (ch. 4).
- Render order: the orientation master first, then the cards reachable from that side, then one person's singles, then the other's, then the closing move. Use the master's frame as the reference for its side (ch. 4).
- A remote, emotionally distant scene keeps people at the room's edges in full shot with extra headroom; close-ups only for contrast (Katz ch. 6).
- Large spaces need scale contrast: repeat near-to-far changes, not wides alone (ch. 6).

## Numbers

- A moving card over 8 s with no named rest: split it or add rests.

## Verify

- Manual: the turn card is listed first in render order; each long move names its rests.
