# Time and order

## Rules

- Each scene and transition has one duration mode: `equal` (screen time = story time, the dialogue default), `compress` (ellipsis cut, montage) or `expand` (crosscutting, repeated angles, slow motion) (*Narration* ch. 6).
- Habitual or repeated action ("every night he...") is a montage of brief moments, not one dramatised scene. A montage summarises one development and works as a transition (*Narration* p. 160).
- A time skip inside a scene needs a cutaway or a dissolve at that join; a plain cut across skipped time reads as a jump (*Classical Hollywood* p. 68).
- Flashback: motivate it as one character's memory, go in and come out on that character's face, and make the return unmistakable. No flashforward in a classical cut (*Narration* pp. 79-81, 161).
- Showing an event twice needs a reason: the second showing reveals something new. Note it as `reveals:` on the repeat (*Narration* p. 80).
- Crosscut strands each get their own place and look (rainy city, hotel, mountains); two strands sharing a set or palette blur (*Labyrinth* p. 49). Strands may run different clocks: near a deadline, shorten the pursuer's cards and stretch the victim's (p. 48).
- One technique per time zone: present plain and locked off, memory handheld and quick, written into the style bible per `timeline` (*Labyrinth* p. 20).
- The more complex the order, the simpler each scene; an out-of-order scene starts and ends on a strongly marked image or line so it joins both ways (*Labyrinth* pp. 20, 49).

## Pitfalls

- An `equal` scene timed from the cards: generated action length is not controlled, so measure from the takes.
- Crosscut and timeline rules matter in longer multi-scene films; a 60 s short rarely needs them.

## Verify

- `edit/cut.md` names each scene's duration mode, and every skip, flashback and repeat with its cue.
