# Walk and talk

## Rules

- The walker leads. The camera pulls back ahead of them and pans to keep them framed (Katz ch. 6).
- The camera starts a moment before the walker and stops just before they reach the mark, so the cut lands on a still frame (ch. 6).
- End the walk on a reason to stop: a doorway, a window, a wall, a hand on a door frame (ch. 6).
- Walking toward a retreating camera reads intimate and raises stakes. Tracking beside the walker introduces a space (ch. 3).
- Two walkers: lock the frame to one. When one stops and the other goes on, choose who the camera stays with; the meaning follows whoever is favoured at the end (ch. 4).
- A long walk with no event turns into decoration. Give it a business beat: touching the window, kicking a stone (ch. 6).
- In remote, emotionally distant scenes, pan with the walker instead of tracking (ch. 6).
- One card per walking segment. A pull-back, a pan and a dolly in one prompt is three moves; split them (ch. 4).

- A car scene is a walk and talk with fixed seats. Options by cost: in-car profile close-ups that cut, a front three-shot through the windscreen, a tracking shot along the windows (ch. 4-5).
- For AI the front three-shot through the windscreen is the master. Mirror and windscreen reflections can stand in for a reverse.
- Keep the travel direction and the sun's side the same on every card of one drive. A return run puts the sun on the wrong side (ch. 5).

## Pitfalls

- A dolly in and back out inside one 8 s card is risky. Split it into two cards that cut on a rest.
- Background drift is the main failure of a long tracking card; keep walks under the model's stable length and cut on a stop.

## Verify

- Manual: each walking card names who leads, where the walk stops and why.
