# Set plan

## Rules

- Plan first, picture second. An art department hands the DP a scaled ground plan with a north arrow before anyone shoots; the DP pre-lights from it and the director draws camera positions on it. A generated set needs the same, or windows and doors move between shots.
- Write `sets/<id>.md` with these parts, in this order:
  1. Size and shape (feet or metres, ceiling height).
  2. Walls by compass name (north, east, south, west, or named views outdoors), each with what stands on it: doors and which way they open, windows and what shows through them, fireplace, hero dressing.
  3. Hero dressing: every object a shot may favour, with its wall and its distance from the wall.
  4. North arrow and the sun's direction for each scene's time of day.
  5. Coverage map: each planned camera, where it stands, which wall it faces. Walls no camera sees need no plate.
  6. Plate log: one line per plate, with its verdict (entry set-check).
- A real place: research its current state, not its famous state. Date every fact and tag it: confirmed by dated reporting, long-standing but not re-checked, or unverified. Buildings change; a remodel two years ago makes the classic photos wrong.
- Draw the plan as a table or a simple text diagram. A rendered plan image is optional; the walls list is what feeds the prompts.
- Leave people out of the set. Portraits, statues and photos of real people are dressing: describe them generically (gilt-framed portraits), never as a named likeness.

## Numbers

- Traditional plan scale: 1/4 inch to 1 foot, 1/8 when space is tight.
- A typical room set needs 3 or 4 walls; an exterior needs the establishing view, the reverse view and one or two details.

## Vocabulary

- ground plan, overhead (plan plus camera and light positions), elevation, wild wall (a wall that comes out so the camera can stand there), hero dressing, set dressing, north arrow.

## Pitfalls

- Skipping the plan and writing one rich paragraph: the model puts the hero wall in every shot (entry wall-strings).
- Mixing the real place's eras: the new remodel in one shot, the old look in the next. Pick one date for the film and research to it.
